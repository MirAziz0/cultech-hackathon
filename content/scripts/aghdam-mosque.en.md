# The mosque that stayed standing

@@ intro@*
Let me take you to a place with a very unusual story.

Imagine a small town in Karabakh, around 150 years ago.

Streets, houses with gardens, shops, the smell of fresh bread in the morning. People going to work, children running to school.

And in the middle of the town, a new mosque is being built.

Welcome to Aghdam, and to its Juma Mosque!

It was built around 1868 to 1870. And the man who designed it was a master named Karbalayi Safikhan Karabakhi.

Remember that name. He was one of the most famous builders of Karabakh. He also built the Govhar Agha mosques in Shusha. So when you look at this mosque, you're looking at the work of a master who shaped the look of a whole region.

@@ courtyard@xix
Now look at it from the outside.

Two minarets, rising on either side like two hands lifted to the sky. Look at the brick patterns on them, the horizontal bands that wrap around them like belts.

Domes above the roof, several of them, one beside the other.

Brick, stone, marble, wood and iron, all working together.

@@ hall@xix
Now let's go inside.

Look around. Stone columns divide the hall. Above you, domed ceilings. And up there, a gallery, a second floor running around the hall.

Imagine a Friday prayer here, a long time ago.

The hall is full. Men below, women in the gallery above. Light comes in through the windows. Someone's child is whispering, an old man is slowly counting his prayer beads.

The master Safikhan planned every column, every dome, every window so that this hall would hold all those people... and feel calm.

@@ courtyard@ruin
Now I have to tell you the hard part of this story.

After 1993, Aghdam was lost and destroyed. The town around the mosque was ruined: the houses, the streets, the gardens. Almost nothing was left standing.

Almost nothing.

Except this mosque.

Imagine looking out over a field of ruins, and in the middle, still standing, two minarets.

The mosque was left in a terrible state. Its roof was gone. Inside, it was damaged and neglected, and for years it was used as a shelter for animals. A journalist who visited in 2011 found it without a roof, open to the sky.

@@ hall@ruin
Think about that for a moment.

A building made for prayer and peace, standing alone for almost thirty years among ruins.

And yet, the walls held. The columns held. The minarets held.

Maybe the master Safikhan built it so well that it simply refused to fall.

@@ courtyard@today
In 2020, Aghdam came back to Azerbaijan. And in 2022, restoration of the mosque began.

Now here's something interesting about how they did it.

They didn't just rebuild it however they liked. Experts studied the original materials: what kind of lime, what kind of stone, how the old masters worked. They strengthened the domes and vaults with lime, the way it was done long ago. They rebuilt the cornice with natural stone.

@@ entrance@today
And while they were clearing the rubble at the entrance, they found something: the old stone pavement, hidden under the debris. The same stones people walked on when they came to pray, more than a hundred years earlier.

In April 2024, the mosque opened its doors again.

@@ hall@today
So now, let's use our time machine.

[Era: XIX century] Here it is, new and bright, in a living town full of people.

[Era: 1993–2020] Here it is, alone among the ruins, roofless, waiting.

[Era: Today] And here it is again, restored, standing in the light.

Three moments. One building.

@@ mihrab@today
You know, sometimes a building is just a building. But sometimes a building becomes a memory, the memory of a whole town that's no longer there.

So here's my question for you.

If you could take one thing from this story and tell it to a friend, what would it be?

The master who built it?

The years it stood alone?

Or those old stones, found again under the rubble at the entrance?

Whatever you choose, tell it. Because places like this stay alive as long as their stories are told.
