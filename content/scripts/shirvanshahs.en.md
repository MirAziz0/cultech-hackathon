# A palace on the hill, where the sea wind still remembers the shahs

@@ intro
Shh... listen for a second.

Can you hear the wind? It comes up from the Caspian Sea, climbs the narrow streets of the Old City and stops right here, at the top of the hill.

Welcome to the Palace of the Shirvanshahs!

@@ court
Long ago, the rulers of Shirvan lived in Shamakhi. But Shamakhi was a city of earthquakes. [CHECK] So the shahs looked for a safer home, and they chose Baku: a city of stone, wind and sea.

And here, on this hill, in the 15th century, they built their palace.

Now look around you. What do you see?

Stone. Everywhere, stone.

This is Absheron limestone, warm, sandy, golden in the afternoon sun. The masters cut it, shaped it and laid it row by row, so carefully that after almost six hundred years the walls are still standing.

@@ portal
Come, let's start with the palace building itself.

Imagine: two floors, and once around fifty rooms. Narrow corridors, small staircases winding up like seashells, rooms opening into one another.

@@ vest
Now picture a guest arriving. He climbs the steps of the high portal, walks through a short passage... and suddenly the room opens up above him, all the way into the dome.

He looks up. And for a moment, he forgets why he came.

That was exactly the idea.

@@ hall
And right in the heart of it, an octagonal hall under a dome.

Why eight sides, you ask?

Maybe because eight walls make a room feel like it's gathering you in. Maybe because the dome sits more beautifully on eight corners than on four. Historians think this hall was used for receptions, and it may be the oldest part of the whole palace.

@@ vest2
But come, let's walk to the most mysterious building in the complex.

The Divankhana.

Look at it: a small octagonal pavilion in the middle of a courtyard, surrounded by arches. And its portal... look closely at the portal. The stone is carved so finely it looks like lace.

Now here's a mystery for you.

Nobody knows for certain what this building was for.

Some say it was the "divan", the hall where the shah held council and judged cases. Others say it was meant to be a mausoleum, a tomb, perhaps for the shah himself.

Imagine standing here with two historians. One says "this was a courtroom!" The other says "no, this was a tomb!"

And the building just stands there, quietly, keeping its secret.

What do you think it was?

@@ corr
Let's go down a little, to the lower courtyard.

Here is the tomb of the Shirvanshahs. Shah Khalilullah I built it in 1435 for his family: his mother and his young son. [CHECK]

Think about that for a second. One of the most powerful men of his time, building a quiet stone room for the people he loved most.

Look up at its portal. Tall, carved, proud. But inside, it's simple and still.

@@ r1
Right next to it stands the Shah Mosque, and its minaret rises above the whole complex. An inscription on the minaret gives the year 1441.

Imagine the call to prayer echoing from up there, over the flat roofs of Baku, all the way down to the sea.

@@ r2
Now, a little further along, there's a small octagonal tomb with a pointed stone roof.

People connect it with Seyid Yahya Bakuvi, a scholar of the shah's court. They say he studied the stars and wrote about science and philosophy. Some people simply call it the "Dervish's tomb".

So in one small palace complex you have power, faith, family... and science.

@@ r3
And now, let me show you something that's easy to miss.

The bathhouse.

Today you'll see only low walls and the shapes of rooms in the ground. It was hidden underground for centuries and found again much later during archaeological work. [CHECK]

But close your eyes and imagine it as it was.

Domes rising just above the ground. Inside, warm steam, the sound of water, light falling in soft beams through small openings in the domes.

The bath was built partly underground for a clever reason: the earth kept the warm rooms warm, and the cool rooms cool.

No electricity, no heating pipes. Just stone, earth, water and the knowledge of the masters.

@@ stair
And before we leave, one more stone story.

Do you see those carved stones lined up in the courtyard? Those are the Bayil stones.

They come from a fortress that once stood near Baku and later disappeared under the waters of the Caspian. For centuries those stones lay underwater, with writing carved on them. Then they were brought up, and now they rest here. [CHECK]

Imagine: words carved by people long ago, sleeping under the sea for hundreds of years, and now waiting for you to read them.

@@ ch3
So, here's my question for you.

A palace isn't just walls and domes. It's councils and prayers, families and scholars, steam and stone, the sea wind and the call to prayer.

If you could walk here for one day, six hundred years ago, who would you want to be?

The shah?

The scholar watching the stars?

Or the master who carved that lace-like portal?

Whoever you choose... the palace is still here, on its hill, waiting to tell you the rest of the story.
