# The Erivan Fortress: A city behind walls, above a river · The Sardar's Palace: Imagine a palace whose very walls dazzle the eye!

@@ intro
Stand here for a moment, at the edge of the cliff.

Look down. Far below, a river is rushing over the stones. That's the Hrazdan.

Now turn around.

In front of you: walls. High, thick, double walls, with towers rising one after another. And beyond them, roofs, domes, minarets, smoke from hundreds of chimneys.

Welcome to the Erivan Fortress!

But this isn't just a fortress. It's a whole little city hidden behind walls.

@@ street
Let's go back to the very beginning.

It's the year 1582. An Ottoman commander, Ferhat Pasha, orders a fortress to be built here, on the high bank of the river. [CHECK: 1582–83]

Why here?

Look at the cliff again. On this side, the river and the steep gorge protect the fortress better than any wall. Enemies simply can't climb up. So the builders only needed walls on the other three sides.

Clever, isn't it? Nature did half the work for them.

But history here never stood still.

In 1679, a powerful earthquake shook the region and the fortress was badly damaged. It was rebuilt again, at the request of the Safavid governor Zal Khan. [CHECK]

So this fortress fell down and stood back up. And that won't be the last time.

@@ gate
Now come, let's walk around it.

The walls run for about one thousand two hundred metres. [CHECK: perimeter ~1,200 m] And they're double: an outer line and an inner line, with towers along them.

To get inside, you had to pass through one of three gates.

The Tabriz Gate. The Shirvan Gate. And the Körpü Gate, the "Bridge Gate", which led down to the bridge over the river.

Each gate had a heavy iron door. And each one had guards.

Now imagine you're a traveller arriving here at sunset. You're tired, your horse is tired. You reach the gate... and you hear the guards calling: "Hurry up, the gates are closing!"

Because at night, the gates were shut.

@@ court
Let's step inside.

Now picture it: narrow streets, about eight hundred houses pressed close together, flat roofs where people dried fruit and slept on hot summer nights. [CHECK: ~800 houses] Soldiers walking by, around two thousand of them lived in the garrison. [CHECK: ~2,000]

There's the sound of a blacksmith's hammer. Someone is selling bread. Children are running between the houses.

And above the roofs, the minarets.

One of them belonged to the Rajab Pasha Mosque, built in 1725. [CHECK]

And another, the Abbas Mirza Mosque, built in the early 19th century. Its façade shone with green and blue glass. Imagine the morning sun hitting that glass. The whole wall would glow.

Today, believe it or not, the frame of this mosque is one of the very few things from the fortress that still stands.

But the most famous building of all is over there, in the north-western corner, right on the edge of the cliff.

The Sardar's Palace.

The home of the rulers. With its famous Hall of Mirrors, its coloured glass windows looking out over the river, its marble pool and fountain, its portraits and paintings.

We'll go inside the palace later. It deserves its own story.

But for now, just look at where it stands: right at the edge. From its windows you could see the river below... and on a clear day, far away on the horizon, the snowy peak of Mount Ararat.

Not a bad view for a palace, is it?

Now I have to tell you how this story changed.

In 1827, during the war between Russia and Persia, the Russian army besieged the fortress and captured it. The walls were damaged, the palace was left half in ruins.

And here's a strange little detail.

After the fortress was taken, a famous play was performed in one of the halls of the palace: "Woe from Wit", a comedy by the Russian writer Alexander Griboyedov. And the author himself was there, watching.

Imagine: a theatre performance, inside a ruler's palace, just after a war.

Then, in 1853, another earthquake hit.

Slowly, the fortress lost its purpose. People moved away. Buildings fell apart. Stones were taken away for other buildings.

In 1865, the land was bought by a merchant, and in the 1880s a brandy factory was built on part of the site. [CHECK]

And by the 1930s, the fortress had been demolished almost completely.

Think about that.

A city behind walls, with gates and guards, mosques and markets, a palace with a Hall of Mirrors... gone.

Today, if you stand where the fortress once was, you'll see a wine factory, a few fragments of old walls, and the frame of the Abbas Mirza Mosque.

And the river. The river is still here, rushing over the same stones.

So how do we know what it looked like?

From drawings by artists who passed through. From descriptions written by travellers. From old plans, old photographs and postcards. And from a model of the fortress kept in a museum. [CHECK]

Every one of those people, without knowing it, was saving a piece of this place for us.

And now, thanks to them, you can stand here again, on the edge of the cliff, and see the walls rise around you.

Of course, we don't know every detail exactly. Some of what you see is a digital reconstruction based on these historical sources.

But the story is real.

So here's my question for you.

If you had lived inside these walls, where would you have spent your evenings?

On a flat roof, watching the sunset over Ararat?

In the market, among the voices and smells?

Or at the edge of the cliff, listening to the river?

@@ south
But come, now let's look at another interesting part of the hall.

There were once special decorated windows here, called "shebeke".

Do you know what shebeke is?

Small wooden pieces are fitted into one another, with coloured glass set between them.

And the most amazing thing is that in the traditional art of shebeke, these pieces can be joined without nails or glue.

Now imagine: all these tiny pieces have to be made so precisely that every one of them fits.

If even one piece were the wrong size, putting the whole pattern together would become difficult.

But the masters did this work so skilfully that the result was both beautiful and an incredibly complex work of art.

And when sunlight passed through these windows, the whole look of the room changed.

Maybe in the morning some colours stood out, and towards noon, as the angle of the light changed, a different scene appeared.

So the same room could look different to you at different hours of the day.

When we think about interior design today, we sometimes believe we've discovered a new idea.
