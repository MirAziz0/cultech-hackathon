## Source conflicts found during research

These came up while collecting facts. Each needs a historian's decision; the app currently hedges them.

- **Shirvanshahs — construction start.** Wikipedia gives a start in 1411 under Ibrahim I, but also says the earliest part dates to the end of the 14th century.
- **Shirvanshahs — 34.15 m² hall area.** Comes from the plan photo on Commons ("Qəbul salonu"), not from a published survey we could read.
- **Shirvanshahs — bay windows, shebeke, alternating masonry courses.** Taken over from the prototype's description; no primary source checked yet.
- **Maiden Tower — wall thickness.** 5 m at the base is consistent; the upper value appears as 3.2–4.2 m, 4 m and 4.5 m in different passages.
- **Maiden Tower — UNESCO dates.** Inscription year given as 2000 (infobox) and 2001 (lead); List in Danger as 2003–2009 and 2004–2009.
- **Maiden Tower — inner stair.** Sources say a staircase "abuts the circular wall"; the Commons photo shows a stair inside a stone passage. The model places it inside the wall thickness as a helix (two storeys per turn) — the exact route is unknown.
- **Maiden Tower — buttress size and the 21 m well's position** (well "discovered on the second floor with an entrance at ground level"). The model puts the well on the ground floor.
- **Erivan Fortress — Sardar's Palace position.** "North-western part" (Erivan Fortress article) vs "north-eastern part" (Sardar's Palace article). The model follows the museum model and the 1827 plan: the palace stands on the gorge (west) side.
- **Erivan Fortress — palace date.** 1798 (Erivan Fortress article) vs "XVI–XVII" (Sardar's Palace infobox).
- **Erivan Fortress — capture date.** The Commons painting title says 1 October 1827 — probably Old Style, which would be 13 October New Style. Confirm which calendar the app should use.
- **Erivan Fortress — Griboyedov performance.** 1827 (Sardar's Palace article) vs a plaque for an 1828 performance (Erivan Fortress article).
- **Erivan Fortress — portraits.** The museum holding them is named as the State Museum of Georgia in one place and the National Gallery of Georgia in another.
- **Erivan Fortress — gates.** Names (Tabriz, Shirvan, Korpu) are sourced; their positions in the model are a hypothesis.
- **Erivan Fortress — Hall of Mirrors dimensions.** Unknown. The model's 9 × 12 m hall with a two-column open front follows Gagarin's lithograph proportions.
- **All monuments — architects.** Usta Rəşid, Usta Fərrux and Usta Kərim are invented representative characters, not historical people, and say so when asked.

### Aghdam Juma Mosque (added October 2026)

- **No measured plan.** All dimensions (about 20 × 25 m footprint, 10 m walls, minarets ≈ 30 m, 6 m domed bays, four free-standing pillars, gallery at 4.6 m) are estimated from Commons photos. The Yukhari Govhar Agha Mosque (26.5 × 21.5 m, same architect) was used as a sanity check.
- **Number of domes.** Wikipedia: "9 (maybe more)"; the 2018 roof photos show a 3×3 grid over the hall. Whether the front block also had domes is unknown (the model gives it a flat roof).
- **19th-century roof.** Not documented. The 1992 photo shows a Soviet-era metal hipped roof; the 2002 stamp also draws a pitched roof. The model's 19th-century state shows exposed lime-plastered domes — a hypothesis.
- **19th-century facade finish.** The marble cladding dates from the 1970s–80s (Monument Watch), so the 19th-century state shows plastered limestone. The blue paint and inscriptions of the iwan are undated.
- **Mihrab decoration.** Painted in 1913 by Muhammad Naghash Tawriz according to Monument Watch (citing qarabag.com) — single source.
- **Women's gallery and stairs.** Monument Watch says the interior is two-storey; the 2024 photo shows a balcony with a wooden balustrade. The U-shaped layout, the piers under it and the stair in the western side room are hypotheses.
- **Damage 1993–2020.** Sources differ in degree: Cornell satellite study — structure unchanged, only the Soviet roofing removed, minor damage; soyqirim.az — minarets dismantled from inside, ceiling broken in several places, inscriptions destroyed; Monument Watch — mihrab and inscriptions intact, minarets undamaged; Report.az (A. Cəfərli) — set on fire several times, minarets damaged, used as an observation post. The app quotes each with attribution. The two holed vaults in the model are placed arbitrarily.
- **First Friday prayer, November 2020.** "After 27 years" (geo.tv) vs "28 years" (Wikipedia).
- **Ashaghi Govhar Agha Mosque date.** 1874–1875 (AZERTAG) vs 1875–1876 (Wikipedia).
- **Courtyard, streets and houses (19th century).** Pure hypothesis; no plan of 19th-century Aghdam was found.
- **Square after 2024.** Hedges, paths and trees approximate the 2024 photos; the layout is not surveyed.
- **Architect persona.** Kərbəlayi Səfixan Qarabaği is a real person; no authentic portrait is known (a 2016 stamp shows a depiction). The app's figure is an original stylised drawing and says so. He speaks first-hand only about events up to 1885 and quotes sources for anything later.

## İrəvan qalası və Sərdar sarayı / Erivan Fortress and the Sardar's Palace (`erivan-fortress`)

### Facts marked for verification (8 of 16)

- [ ] **ef-garrison** (standing / observed) — The fortress held a garrison of about 2,000 soldiers and some 800 houses.
  - source: [Wikipedia: Erivan Fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erivan_Fortress)
- [ ] **ef-1827** (standing / observed) — In the Russo-Persian War of 1826–1828 the fortress was besieged and taken by General Paskevich's army in October 1827.
  - source: [Wikipedia: Erivan Fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erivan_Fortress)
  - source: [Wikimedia Commons: Siege of Erivan Fortress on 1 October 1827](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Siege_of_Erivan_Fortress_on_1_October_1827.jpg)
- [ ] **ef-today** (standing / observed) — In 1865 the merchant Nerses Tairyants bought the site and in the 1880s built a brandy factory there. The fortress was demolished by the 1930s; today the Ararat wine factory stands on the site, and some wall remnants survive.
  - source: [Wikipedia: Erivan Fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erivan_Fortress)
- [ ] **sp-palace** (evidence-based reconstruction) — The Sardar's Palace was the residence of the ruler of the Erivan Khanate, in the part of the fortress overlooking the gorge on the river's left bank. One source dates it to 1798; others give an earlier date.
  - source: [Wikipedia: Sardar's Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardar%27s_Palace)
  - source: [Wikipedia: Erivan Fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erivan_Fortress)
- [ ] **sp-harem** (evidence-based reconstruction) — According to the sources the palace harem measured about 61 × 38 m, and its bath had a pool about 32 m long and 9 m wide.
  - source: [Wikipedia: Erivan Fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erivan_Fortress)
- [ ] **sp-portraits** (standing / observed) — The Hall of Mirrors had eight large portraits. In the 1850s Mirza Kadym Erivani painted four oil portraits of about 1 × 2 m for the restored palace: Fath-Ali Shah, crown prince Abbas Mirza, Sardar Huseyn-Quli Khan and his brother. They were removed in 1914 and are now in a museum collection in Georgia.
  - source: [Wikipedia: Sardar's Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardar%27s_Palace)
- [ ] **sp-griboyedov** (standing / observed) — After the fortress was taken, Russian officers staged Alexander Griboyedov's comedy “Woe from Wit” in one of the palace rooms, with Griboyedov himself taking part in the staging.
  - source: [Wikipedia: Sardar's Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardar%27s_Palace)
  - source: [Wikipedia: Erivan Fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erivan_Fortress)
- [ ] **sp-tiles** (standing / observed) — Some Qajar-period items from the palace were restored and shown for the first time in Baku, at the Heydar Aliyev Center, in 2019.
  - source: [Wikipedia: Sardar's Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardar%27s_Palace)
